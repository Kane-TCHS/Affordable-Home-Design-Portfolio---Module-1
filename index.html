01 / UNDERSTAND THE CLIENT

Start with people, then rooms.

Before drawing walls, review your client survey to identify who will use the home, how they will live in it, and which needs and preferences should guide your design.

Your group is designing an affordable, single-story home for a Florida family. Use your Client Survey, especially “Anything else we should know?”, to guide the arrangement. Every family in this project needs three bedrooms and two full bathrooms.

Needs before wants: make two lists on scratch paper. First make space for what the family needs to live safely and comfortably. Then consider preferences that fit within the project limits.

Your project limits

  • Three bedrooms and two full bathrooms
  • Single story, with no loft, garage, or carport
  • Outside house area of 1,200 sq ft or less; exclude the porch or patio
  • Kitchen, living, dining, laundry, storage, entry, and porch or patio
  • At least one no-step entrance
How do I assign bedrooms?

A couple shares a room. Every other adult gets their own room. Children do not share with adults, and no more than two children share a room. A boy and girl need separate rooms when the older child is six or older. Use the survey to assign the three bedrooms.

Which source should I follow?

Use your assignment and Module 1 Scoring Checklist for project requirements. This guide helps you plan and draw; it does not replace those instructions.

02 / ORGANIZE RELATIONSHIPS

Connect spaces before drawing walls.

Color shows how each space is used. It helps you group spaces that work together and notice where noisy or shared activities meet quiet, personal areas.

Public space

Where the household gathers and welcomes guests. These are the shared areas for everyday activities and visiting.

Examples: living room, dining area, kitchen, and entry.

“Public” does not mean anyone can enter the home. It means these spaces are generally appropriate for guests.

Private space

Where people need privacy for personal activities. These spaces support sleeping, bathing, dressing, and retreating from shared activity.

Examples: bedrooms and bathrooms.

A bathroom is still private space even when guests use it: the activity requires privacy.

Service space

Where household tasks, storage, and equipment are located. These spaces support the operation and upkeep of the home.

Examples: laundry, storage closets, and utility areas.

For this project, classify the kitchen as public space because it supports shared household activity, even though cooking is a household task.

A bubble diagram shows which spaces should be near or connected to one another. Use one labeled bubble for every space, including the hall, entry, laundry, storage, and porch or patio. Larger spaces get larger bubbles. It is not a measured floor plan.

How should I draw the connections?

Thick lines: direct walking connections. Thin lines: spaces should be near each other but do not need a door between them. Mark the front door with an arrow from outside.

How do I choose a color for a space?

Use the space’s main purpose, not just who owns it. Follow the project categories: kitchen is public, bathrooms are private, and laundry is service. Use the same color for every bubble in a category and include a labeled color key. You may choose your own colors.

For a space with more than one use, choose its main purpose and add a short label if needed. A storage closet is service space even when it sits inside a private bedroom.

In this example, gray identifies the shared hall and porch/patio separately so you can follow the connections; it is not a fourth use category. In your diagram, consider what these spaces serve: an entry porch typically belongs with public spaces, while a hall serving only bedrooms belongs with private spaces.

Try two or three arrangements in pencil. Choose the one that best responds to your client’s needs, including their “Anything else” request.

03 / GIVE THE SPACES SHAPE

Turn relationships into a rough layout.

Use a ruler and start with the outside walls. Your sketch does not have to be to scale, but rooms should look roughly proportional. Toggle the example layers to see what each adds.

Plan access early: every bedroom should reach a bathroom without passing through another bedroom. Keep the route from the front door to the kitchen out of private rooms.
  1. Block out rooms. Draw thicker double lines for exterior walls and thinner double lines for interior walls.
  2. Place openings. Leave wall gaps for doors and show their swings. Draw windows with three thin parallel lines in the wall. Every bedroom needs at least one window. Use the symbol key below for examples.
  3. Add the required basics. Show bedroom closets, kitchen counters, bathroom fixtures, and a washer and dryer. Furniture can help test space.
  4. Walk the plan. Trace ordinary trips and look for conflicts.

This diagram demonstrates layout and drawing layers, not a finished design to copy. Your sketch must also include room sizes, overall dimensions, labeled door and hall widths, and the clear bathroom turning circle.

Public & private

Keep guest activities near shared spaces. Consider noise and sightlines into bedrooms and bathrooms.

Circulation

Circulation is the space people use to move. Furniture and door swings should not block those routes.

Room fit

A room’s area alone does not prove it works. Its shape, wall lengths, and openings affect what fits.

DRAWING REFERENCE

Use symbols people can read.

Floor-plan symbols show objects from above. Use these simplified examples when adding openings, fixtures, appliances, and closets to your sketch. Dark blocks in the examples represent walls; in your floor plan, use the double-line wall convention.

Start with the required basics: doors, bedroom windows, bedroom closets, kitchen counters, bathroom fixtures, and a washer and dryer. Other symbols are available when they apply to your design. Label anything that could be unclear.

Showing all 20 symbols.

Hinged door

Leave a wall gap. Draw the door leaf from its hinge and a quarter-circle arc to show its swing. Keep the swing clear.

Sliding door

Draw overlapping panels in the opening. The panels slide alongside one another; there is no hinged swing arc.

Bifold door

Draw connected panels as angled lines. Often used for closets; leave space in front for the folded panels.

Pocket door

Show the door sliding into a wall pocket. The wall beside the opening must have room for the panel.

Window: basic plan symbol

Use three thin parallel lines across a gap in the exterior wall, as required in the assignment. Include at least one window in every bedroom.

Casement window

A casement opens on a side hinge. A panel and swing indication distinguish it from a fixed window. Label the type if needed.

Double-hung window

The sashes move vertically. The plan symbol can resemble a fixed window, so use a label or window schedule to identify the type.

Refrigerator

Draw a rectangle labeled REF. Place it by a wall or cabinet run, with room to open the appliance door.

Range / stove

Draw a rectangle with four burner circles. Place it in the kitchen cabinet layout.

Dishwasher

Label the under-counter rectangle DW. Dashed lines indicate the appliance is beneath the countertop. Place it near the sink if included.

Kitchen sink

Draw the basin inside the counter outline. Leave usable counter space beside it.

Base & upper cabinets

Outline base cabinets with solid lines. Show overhead cabinets with dashed lines and label them where needed. Keep the counter run against its intended wall.

Bathtub

Draw a long rectangle with a rounded basin inside. Position it against the bathroom wall and keep it outside the required turning circle.

Shower

Draw a square or rectangle with diagonal lines toward the drain. Use dimensions that fit the design, and keep the required turning circle clear.

Toilet

Draw a small rectangular tank against the wall and an oval bowl projecting into the room. Keep usable space in front.

Vanity & bathroom sink

Draw the cabinet rectangle and basin inside it. The cabinet must stay outside the required 5-foot turning circle.

Washing machine

Draw a rectangle labeled W. Show both the washer and dryer in the laundry space, with room to access them.

Dryer

Draw a rectangle labeled D. Show both the washer and dryer in the laundry space, with room to access them.

Water heater

For a tank-type unit, use a circle labeled WH. Include it if your design identifies a utility location; it is not an extra Module 1 submission requirement.

Closet shelf & rod

Draw the closet enclosure against a room wall. Add a shelf line and a dashed rod line. The assignment gives 2 feet as a starting closet depth; show how it opens into the room.

Symbols are enlarged for clarity and are not drawn to a shared scale. Size each item to fit your plan. Appliance dimensions vary; use the dimensions of your selected item when checking fit. This project has no stairs or lofts.

04 / DIMENSIONS & ROOM TABLE

Label sizes and check the total area.

Label every room with its name and dimensions, such as BEDROOM 2 – 10′ × 11′. Show overall dimensions on one long side and one short side. For a rectangular house, outside length × outside width must be no more than 1,200 sq ft. For an L shape, split the footprint into rectangles and add their areas. Exclude the porch or patio.

Add a room table listing every room, its dimensions, and its area. Example: Bedroom 2 | 10′ × 11′ | 110 sq ft. Put the table on the sketch page or a separate portfolio page.

Optional scale: your assignment allows a proportional sketch. If you choose a scale, drawing length (in.) = real length (ft) × scale (in. per ft).

The room table total is not the outside house area. Walls and spaces not separately counted in the table also use area. Check the outside footprint separately; avoid counting closets or other spaces twice.
What about wall thickness?

Walls take up space. Decide whether a dimension measures the clear inside room, a wall centerline, or the outside of the building, and label it consistently. Adding room areas does not automatically give the building’s total footprint.

When should I use architectural software?

Your Revit model will be built from your floor plan sketch. Make the sketch and its dimensions clear now so your group has a useful starting point for the later model.

PROJECT CHECKS

Make the required clearances visible.

Doors & entrance

Provide at least one no-step entrance. Follow the assignment’s doorway requirement of at least 34″ clear. Label at least one door 3′ and use that door width throughout.

Door size and clear opening are different measurements; the open door and frame reduce the clear passage.

Hallways

Label each hallway width. Use at least 42″ (3½′) where doors open off the hallway, or 40″ where none do, as specified in the assignment.

Bathroom turning space

In one bathroom, draw and label a 5′ turning circle. Keep toilets, tubs, and cabinets outside that circle. Both bathrooms must include a tub or shower, toilet, and sink.

An 8′ × 8′ room is only a starting point; test the actual fixture arrangement and clear space.
